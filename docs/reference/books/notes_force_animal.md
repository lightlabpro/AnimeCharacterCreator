# FORCE: Animal Drawing - Michael D. Mattesi (Focal 2011, 221 pp) - reading notes (q20)

Read: full text layer (every page, 2 chunks) + every page image (25 3x3 sheets). Animal locomotion and design
for animators. Little direct face content; relevant parts are the design chapter and the face designs.

## Core content
- Preface: contrast creates interest; hierarchy (pose, then limbs, then digits; or face first if the story is
  there); one line = one idea; directional and applied force, rhythm, 45 degrees is the most dramatic angle; the
  forceful shape is straight against curve, never mirrored (pipe, sausage, pinch); the concave curve from skin
  stretched joint to joint is allowed but sparingly; don't merge a concave and a convex line into one (p17-26).
- Ch1 the force animal: compared with a human the quadruped has one fewer directional force because the scapula
  has no clavicle; the ribcage hangs between the forelimbs; the "force animal" silhouette in three straight-to-
  curve sections (hip up, ribcage down, neck and head up); plantigrade / digitigrade / unguligrade; walk cycle
  (rear leg "kicks" the front leg forward), lead foot, folding-chair parallel limbs (p27-46).
- Ch2-4 class by class: bears, raccoons, kangaroos, rodents, lizard, primates (have clavicles, so no force animal
  shape), dogs, fox, wolf, hyena, cats, lion, cheetah, ocelot, leopard, tiger, elephants, birds, horses, zebras,
  rhinos, deer, elk, antelopes, giraffes; plus face designs - grizzly (small eyes, long sweeping muzzle), raccoon
  mask in front and 3/4, chimp faces pushed in brow / jaw / lips, lion and tiger faces read through plane changes
  (p47-196).
- Ch5 design: the law of thirds - halving creates symmetry and kills interest, divide into thirds both ways;
  bear face designed from a long vertical ellipse with the near eye larger for depth; proportion stretches
  create different characters; colour and contrast to direct focus (p197-214).

## What this means for head_mhs3_n56 (q20)
- Law of thirds supports avoiding a face split in half: our lower face should not equal the upper face. Anime
  faces put the eye line well below the head's midpoint and keep the lower face short (agrees with Hart p19).
- In 3/4 the near eye is drawn larger than the far eye for depth (p201), consistent with Cooney p69.
